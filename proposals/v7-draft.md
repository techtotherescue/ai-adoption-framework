# AI Adoption Framework for Social Impact Organizations
## Version 7 — Five Stages, Adoption Loop, and Supportable Capacity Domains

*Updated: 2026-07-31*

> Transcribed on 2026-08-03 from the authors' document of 2026-07-31.
> Supersedes v3, which is not published here.

## 1. The Core Thesis

AI adoption in social impact organizations is not primarily a tool-selection or software-construction problem. Social impact organizations are nonprofits, social enterprises, and impact ventures.

**AI adoption in social impact organizations is an operating-model transition.**

AI tools and prototypes are becoming easier and cheaper to produce. The scarce capabilities are increasingly:

- selecting problems and workflows worth changing;
- understanding users and validating needs;
- preparing organizational data and context;
- redesigning workflows, jobs, and accountability;
- deploying securely and reliably;
- governing risk in proportion to consequence;
- maintaining systems and managing dependency;
- measuring whether outcomes improve;
- feeding evidence into the next iteration.

The central distinction is therefore:

**AI use is not the same as AI-enabled operating-model change.**

The framework helps determine which one is happening, what should happen next, and what support is appropriate.

## 2. How To Read The Framework

Version 7 preserves the three dimensions that v5 separated.

### Dimension 1: Adoption Stage

The adoption stage describes how deeply and reliably the organization can integrate AI into its work.

| Stage | Name | Core capability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Resisting | Building trust that AI can fit the mission without harming the human work. |
| 2 | Exploring | Learning through fragmented individual and team experimentation. |
| 3 | Systematizing | Building an AI adoption operating system and running deliberate adoption loops. |
| 4 | Innovating | Reliably operating redesigned mission-critical workflows around AI. |
| 5 | Enabling | Providing shared infrastructure, tools, standards, evidence, or capacity others can depend on. |

### Dimension 2: Deployment Scope

Deployment scope describes where AI acts and who is exposed to it.

| Scope | Description |
| --- | --- |
| None | No meaningful AI use. |
| Individual | Personal productivity and one-off assistance. |
| Shared internal | Repeatable team or organizational workflows. |
| Mission-critical internal | Workflows essential to delivery, quality, allocation, or organizational performance. |
| Beneficiary/external | AI directly affects beneficiaries, users, applicants, partners, or public information. |
| Ecosystem-facing | Other organizations depend on shared tools, data, standards, APIs, or services. |

Deployment scope does not determine the stage by itself. A superficial external chatbot is not necessarily Stage 4. A deeply redesigned internal case-management or service-allocation workflow may be Stage 4 if it meets the evidence, reliability, ownership, and governance requirements.

### Dimension 3: Risk Exposure

Risk exposure describes the consequences if the AI-supported workflow is wrong, unavailable, biased, insecure, or misused.

| Level | Typical characteristics |
| --- | --- |
| Low | Reversible internal assistance with limited sensitive data and human review. |
| Moderate | Shared workflows, sensitive organizational data, or decisions that affect operational quality. |
| High | Consequential decisions, vulnerable populations, sensitive personal data, or limited reversibility. |
| Critical | Potential effects on safety, legal rights, essential services, freedom, or severe and difficult-to-reverse harm. |

Risk must be assessed independently of stage. More advanced organizations may operate low-risk workflows. Early-stage organizations may experiment with dangerously high-risk ideas.

### The Practical Reading

A useful diagnosis should say:

> Stage 3 Hybrid organization. Mission-critical internal pilot. Moderate risk. Main bottlenecks: workflow redesign, evidence, and vendor exit readiness.

This is more useful than a stage label alone.

## 3. Organizational Aspiration Profiles

The framework should help an organization choose a healthy aspiration, not push everyone up the ladder.

### 1. Human-To-Human / Last-Mile Organizations

These organizations create value primarily through trust, presence, relationships, local context, and human service delivery.

**Healthy aspiration:** often Stage 3, and sometimes Stage 4 for selected internal or mission-critical workflows.

They should become AI-literate and AI-enabled where it strengthens the human work. They may not need to build beneficiary-facing AI tools.

**Risk if pushed too far:** they waste time and money trying to become technology organizations when their comparative advantage is human proximity.

### 2. Hybrid Organizations

These organizations deliver human services but also have workflows where custom AI can improve beneficiary outcomes, operational quality, or reach.

**Healthy aspiration:** usually Stage 3 or Stage 4.

They should redesign selected mission-critical workflows while relying on external infrastructure or expert partners for capabilities they should not own alone.

**Risk if pushed too far:** they overbuild infrastructure instead of improving the few workflows where AI creates meaningful value.

### 3. Tech-Focused / Infrastructure Organizations

These organizations have strong technical capacity, reusable data or knowledge assets, and a natural role in enabling others.

**Healthy aspiration:** Stage 4 or Stage 5.

They can operate reliable tools for their own users and, where mission fit and demand are strong, turn those capabilities into infrastructure others use.

**Risk if pushed too narrowly:** they become isolated builders and fail to distribute what they create through trusted implementation and adoption partners.

## 4. The Adoption Loop

Stages describe organizational capacity. The Adoption Loop describes the repeatable work through which capacity is built.

### Step 1: Build Literacy, Trust, and Permission

- Understand practical capabilities and limits.
- Surface fears about jobs, dignity, privacy, bias, and mission fit.
- Give staff permission to experiment safely.
- Use trusted peer examples rather than inevitability narratives.

### Step 2: Select a Meaningful Workflow

- Start with a real pain point or opportunity.
- Understand the existing workflow before adding AI.
- Define the users, intended outcome, baseline, and constraints.
- Ask whether AI is better than process improvement, training, CRM configuration, automation, or no intervention.

### Step 3: Experiment Safely

- Use the smallest experiment that can produce useful learning.
- Apply an initial data, privacy, and consequence screen before experimentation.
- Keep accountable humans involved.
- Avoid exposing beneficiaries to an unvalidated system.

### Step 4: Observe Where The System Breaks

Observe:

- quality and reliability;
- cost and consumption;
- data and context gaps;
- staff and user behavior;
- bias and exclusion;
- exceptions and edge cases;
- dependency on vendors, partners, or specific people;
- unintended effects.

### Step 5: Redesign The Workflow And Roles

- Decide which tasks AI performs, supports, or should not touch.
- Define human review, judgment, escalation, and appeal.
- Redesign jobs and handoffs rather than adding AI on top of a broken process.
- Involve affected staff and users.
- Provide training and change support.

### Step 6: Deploy With Proportional Governance

- Increase safeguards in proportion to risk and exposure.
- Establish ownership, access rules, monitoring, rollback, and failure handling.
- Confirm maintenance and financial sustainability.
- Confirm data portability and meaningful exit paths.

Governance begins before experimentation. Step 6 formalizes and strengthens it for repeatable operation.

### Step 7: Measure Outcomes And Feed Learning Back

- Compare performance to the baseline.
- Measure adoption and behavior, not only availability.
- Examine failures and unintended outcomes.
- Decide whether to improve, expand, constrain, replace, or stop the workflow.
- Feed evidence into the next loop.

The Adoption Loop is progressive, not a minimum checklist every organization must complete immediately. Earlier-stage organizations will usually run only part of it, and that is healthy when the purpose is learning rather than premature deployment:

- **Stage 1** organizations mainly build trust, literacy, and permission.
- **Stage 2** organizations select workflows, experiment, and observe where the system breaks.
- **Stage 3** organizations begin completing the full loop on selected workflows, although evidence, maintenance, or repeatability may still be immature.
- **Stage 4** organizations can run the full loop reliably for mission-critical workflows.
- **Stage 5** organizations extend the loop across organizational boundaries and use evidence from adopters to improve shared infrastructure or capacity.

Organizations may run the loop many times while remaining at the same stage. Stage progression reflects their growing ability to complete it reliably across meaningful workflows — not the completion of one pilot.

## 5. Stage Profiles

## Stage 1: Resisting

Organizations are holding back from AI because they do not yet trust its fit with their mission, people, data, or values.

### What Defines This Stage

- Deep skepticism or active avoidance of AI tools.
- Concerns about job replacement, ethics, privacy, bias, dignity, or mission authenticity.
- Little direct use inside the organization.
- AI is understood mainly as a threat, distraction, or hype cycle.

### Key Bottleneck

**Trust and worldview.**

The organization does not yet believe AI can support its mission without damaging what makes the work valuable.

### Adoption Loop Focus

Primarily Step 1: literacy, trust, and permission.

### Context Maturity

There is no trusted view of what organizational data, knowledge, values, or lived experience could be used safely as AI context.

### What They Need

- Safe exposure to peers using AI responsibly.
- Values-based examples from their sector.
- Permission to experiment without shame or pressure.
- Evidence that AI can enhance rather than replace human judgment.
- Lightweight, reversible use cases before strategy pressure.
- Practical answers to privacy and mission concerns.

### What Backfires

- AI inevitability narratives.
- Technical-only training.
- Forced adoption mandates.
- Treating concerns as ignorance.
- Pushing beneficiary-facing AI before trust exists.

### Observable Indicators

- **Leadership and ownership:** no owner; AI is avoided or treated as someone else's issue.
- **People, workflows, and change:** low literacy; no workflow participation or redesign.
- **Data and organizational context:** no shared view of what data, knowledge, values, or policies AI could use safely.
- **Technology, implementation, and resilience:** technical options are unknown or disconnected from adoption needs.
- **Governance, risk, and accountability:** concerns exist, but there is no practical guidance or accountable decision-maker.
- **Evidence and learning:** no direct organizational evidence; beliefs come mainly from headlines or fear.
- **Resources and sustainability:** no allocated time or budget.

## Stage 2: Exploring

Individuals and teams are trying AI and discovering useful gains without shared direction.

### What Defines This Stage

- Individuals use general AI tools for writing, research, summarization, reports, administration, analysis, or ideation.
- Usage is fragmented and uneven.
- Early champions share wins informally.
- The organization has no coherent operating model.
- Experiments may occur in real workflows but are not yet repeatable organizational practice.

### Key Bottleneck

**Coordination and coherence.**

The organization has enough curiosity to experiment but not enough structure to know what is safe, useful, repeatable, or worth scaling.

### Adoption Loop Focus

Steps 1–4: build confidence, select workflows, experiment safely, and learn where quality, risk, cost, and context break.

### Context Maturity

Context is mostly personal: individual prompts, files, notes, and informal prompt libraries. It is rarely maintained or governed as an organizational asset.

### What They Need

- Internal showcasing of practical use cases and failures.
- Basic safe-use and data-handling norms.
- A map of current use.
- Lightweight workflow discovery.
- Simple tool guidance.
- An initial consequence and risk screen.
- A learning rhythm.
- A path toward a named owner if use keeps spreading.

### What Backfires

- Heavy strategy documents.
- Expensive enterprise tools before use cases are clear.
- Custom builds before workflow and context readiness.
- Beneficiary-facing experiments without safeguards.
- Pro-bono projects that skip internal ownership.
- Treating experimentation volume as adoption maturity.

### Observable Indicators

- **Leadership and ownership:** no formal owner; informal champions may exist.
- **People, workflows, and change:** literacy is uneven and concentrated in enthusiasts; AI is added to tasks rather than used to redesign workflows.
- **Data and organizational context:** personal prompts and files; no maintained shared context.
- **Technology, implementation, and resilience:** free tools, individual subscriptions, or early dependence on one enthusiastic person or partner.
- **Governance, risk, and accountability:** basic awareness with little practical guidance or explicit human accountability.
- **Evidence and learning:** anecdotal usefulness, time savings, and documented failures; no baseline.
- **Resources and sustainability:** little protected time and no shared implementation budget.

### Pro-Bono Rule Of Thumb

At Stage 2, pro-bono support should focus on exploration, training, workflow mapping, discovery, and readiness. It should not build mission-critical systems.

## Stage 3: Systematizing

Organizations are turning scattered AI use into a shared AI adoption operating system.

### What Defines This Stage

- AI use becomes expected in selected areas rather than exceptional.
- The organization standardizes tools, licenses, practices, and safe-use norms.
- A named AI owner, champion, or adoption group has time and authority.
- The organization prioritizes workflows deliberately.
- It can run the Adoption Loop on selected workflows.
- Most adoption remains internal, but pilots may include mission-critical work if risk and ownership are appropriate.

### Key Bottleneck

**AI adoption operating system.**

The organization needs ownership, rhythm, shared tools, context, workflow redesign capability, evidence practices, technical support, and a practical roadmap.

### Adoption Loop Focus

The organization can complete all seven steps on selected internal workflows, although evidence, maintenance, or repeatability may still be immature.

### Context Maturity

The organization has shared, organized context: approved knowledge sources, reusable prompts or assistants, basic access norms, a data/context inventory, and an emerging maintenance owner.

Context includes:

- structured and unstructured data;
- policies and procedures;
- strategy and program knowledge;
- quality criteria;
- mission constraints and organizational values;
- domain expertise and lived experience.

### Stage 3 Completion Signals

A solid Stage 3 organization should have most of the following. It must have a named AI owner, champion, or adoption group with time and authority. Without one, it cannot be placed above Stage 2.

- AI adoption owner with time and authority.
- Shared licenses or approved tool stack.
- Team education and learning rhythm.
- Safe-use and data-handling norms.
- Workflow and use-case prioritization.
- Baseline and intended outcome for priority workflows.
- Data/context inventory and maintenance responsibility.
- Explicit map of changing tasks, roles, review, and exceptions.
- Technical support model.
- Vendor and partner dependency view.
- Budget, credits, and protected implementation time.
- Regular implementation cadence.
- Criteria for deciding which workflows are ready for operational deployment.

### What They Need

- A 90-day AI adoption sprint or equivalent.
- AI adoption owner with decision-maker sponsorship.
- Shared tool decisions.
- Internal learning and evidence rhythm.
- Workflow and job redesign support.
- Data/context organization.
- Basic acceptable-use and data-handling guidance.
- Dependency and exit-readiness review.
- Pilot-readiness criteria.
- Change-management support.
- A decision about where agents or agentic interfaces are justified, if anywhere.

### Agentic Readiness At Stage 3

Stage 3 organizations may assess:

- whether the workflow is stable enough for agentic operation;
- what permissions an agent would need;
- what actions must require human confirmation;
- how activity would be observed and audited;
- whether agentic complexity produces enough value to justify it.

Building agents is not required for Stage 3 completion.

### What Backfires

- One-off training without ownership or implementation cadence.
- Treating AI as only an IT project.
- Moving directly from scattered experiments to a flagship build.
- Automating a broken workflow without redesigning roles.
- Relying on a pro-bono partner while internal ownership is weak.
- Buying a shared tool without addressing context, behavior, and learning.

### Observable Indicators

- **Leadership and ownership:** named AI coordinator, owner, or adoption group with emerging authority and rhythm.
- **People, workflows, and change:** broad basic fluency; priority workflows are mapped and tasks, roles, review, and exceptions are being redesigned.
- **Data and organizational context:** shared prompts, knowledge sources, inventory, access norms, and maintenance responsibility are emerging.
- **Technology, implementation, and resilience:** enough capacity to manage tools and pilots; key vendors and partners are known, with portability and exit options beginning to be assessed.
- **Governance, risk, and accountability:** acceptable-use guidance, basic risk screening, and named human accountability.
- **Evidence and learning:** baselines and intended outcomes exist for priority workflows; regular learning reviews begin.
- **Resources and sustainability:** tool budget, credits, protected implementation time, and a reliable partner model are emerging.

### Pro-Bono Rule Of Thumb

At Stage 3, pro-bono support can help run pilots only if the organization has:

- an internal owner;
- decision-maker commitment;
- protected time;
- usable data/context;
- a clear workflow and intended outcome;
- a risk screen;
- an evidence plan;
- a handoff and exit plan.

Without those conditions, pro-bono support often creates an artifact the organization cannot adopt.

## Stage 4: Innovating

Organizations have redesigned and reliably operate one or more mission-critical workflows around AI capabilities.

The workflows may be internal or external. Stage 4 is defined by operational transformation, evidence, reliability, ownership, and accountability — not by beneficiary exposure alone.

### What Defines This Stage

- AI is integral to a workflow that matters to mission delivery or organizational performance.
- The workflow is redesigned rather than merely supplemented with an AI tool.
- Human roles, review, escalation, and exception handling are explicit.
- The workflow operates repeatedly outside a temporary demo environment.
- Evidence is used to improve the workflow.
- Governance, maintenance, dependency, and failure handling are active operating practices.
- Beneficiary-facing deployment may exist, but it is not required.

### What Does Not Qualify

- A prototype nobody uses.
- A beneficiary-facing chatbot with no evidence, maintenance, or owner.
- A one-off automation dependent on one staff member.
- A vendor product that is available but has not changed the workflow.
- A high-risk deployment without accountable human review and failure handling.

### Key Bottleneck

**Reliable and responsible workflow transformation.**

The challenge is turning demonstrated value into a maintained operating capability that improves outcomes without creating unacceptable harm or dependency.

### Adoption Loop Focus

The organization runs full Adoption Loops continuously: monitoring real operation, learning from outcomes and failures, and improving or retiring workflows.

### Context Maturity

Context is a maintained and governed organizational asset integrated into mission-critical workflows. It has:

- authoritative sources;
- ownership and update cadence;
- access and consent rules;
- quality criteria;
- traceability where relevant;
- mission and values constraints;
- evaluation data.

### Stage 4 Operating Gates

Before a mission-critical AI workflow is considered operationally ready, the organization should have:

- a defined user need and intended outcome;
- a baseline and evaluation plan;
- accountable AI adoption and technical ownership;
- a redesigned workflow and role model;
- data/context ownership and quality controls;
- a risk and consequence assessment;
- human review, escalation, exception, and appeal rules where relevant;
- security and privacy stance;
- monitoring after deployment;
- staff or user acceptability checks;
- maintenance and incident ownership;
- cost and consumption controls;
- vendor, model, data, and partner dependency review;
- portability, rollback, or migration path;
- a decision process for continuation, expansion, constraint, or retirement.

Stage 4 requires what defines this stage (beneficiary-facing deployment remains optional), accountable AI adoption and technical ownership, and data/context ownership and quality controls. The other gates are a readiness checklist. An unmet gate is a gap to close (§6) and does not lower the stage.

"Governance, maintenance, dependency, and failure handling are active operating practices" describes the organization's practice. It is not met or failed by whether every workflow meets every gate.

A workflow matters to mission delivery or organizational performance when it passes this test: if it failed or went wrong for a week, the people the organization serves would be harmed, or a core operation would stop. Allocating emergency food parcels passes. Drafting a newsletter does not. Writing grant applications passes only when the organization depends on those grants and AI is integral to how they are written.

### Additional Gates For Beneficiary Or External Exposure

When AI affects beneficiaries, users, applicants, public information, or vulnerable groups, the organization should also have:

- participation by affected users or credible representatives;
- documented limits on automated decisions;
- qualified and accountable human review;
- a clear method to question, correct, or appeal outcomes where relevant;
- testing for exclusion, bias, and differential harm;
- communication that makes the role of AI understandable;
- safeguards appropriate to the political and surveillance environment.

These gates never lower the stage. When AI affects the people the organization serves, a missing gate makes its domain red (§6). An appeal route is relevant when AI makes or shapes a decision about a specific person: who receives help, who is refused, or in what order people are served.

### Agentic Systems At Stage 4

Agents or agent-facing services should be deployed only when the use case justifies the added complexity and the organization has:

- tightly scoped permissions;
- human confirmation for consequential actions;
- observability and auditability;
- cost and consumption limits;
- failure and recovery handling;
- secure credential management;
- a reliable way to disable or replace the system.

Agentic operation is optional. A reliable non-agentic workflow may be the better design.

### What They Need

- Deployment and workflow-integration support.
- User research and workflow validation.
- Responsible-AI and human-accountability design.
- Maintained data/context assets.
- Testing and outcome evaluation.
- Staff and user adoption strategy.
- Change-management and job-transition support.
- Senior technical mentorship and quality assurance.
- Maintenance, incident, and financial model.
- Dependency management and exit readiness.

### What Backfires

- Generic boot camps.
- Junior developers as the main support for high-risk systems.
- Isolated demos disconnected from real workflows.
- Deploying to users before safety, privacy, evaluation, and maintenance are clear.
- Measuring output volume instead of workflow or mission outcomes.
- Treating pro-bono delivery as a substitute for ownership.
- Assuming open source or self-hosting automatically solves trust and control.

### Observable Indicators

- **Leadership and ownership:** an adoption owner and technical owner or reliable senior partner share clear accountability.
- **People, workflows, and change:** staff can operate, question, and improve the workflow; roles, human judgment, escalation, and exceptions are operationalized.
- **Data and organizational context:** a maintained context asset has clear ownership, governance, quality controls, and evaluation data.
- **Technology, implementation, and resilience:** integration, testing, maintenance, senior quality assurance, known cost exposure, portability, fallback, and an exit path are in place.
- **Governance, risk, and accountability:** active controls, accountable humans, monitoring, appeals where relevant, and incident handling match the consequences.
- **Evidence and learning:** operational baselines, adoption data, outcome evidence, failure reviews, and a regular improvement cadence.
- **Resources and sustainability:** implementation resources and a credible maintenance and operating-cost path.

### Pro-Bono Rule Of Thumb

At Stage 4, pro-bono support is acceptable only with strong quality assurance:

- senior technical quality;
- clear scope and accountability;
- testing and evidence plan;
- handoff and documentation;
- maintenance and incident plan;
- governance requirements;
- named technical accountability;
- explicit post-program ownership;
- continuity if the partner leaves.

For high-risk AI, a few volunteers building something is not enough.

## Stage 5: Enabling

Organizations provide shared tools, infrastructure, standards, context, evidence, or implementation capacity that other organizations can depend on.

### What Defines This Stage

- AI capacity serves beyond the organization's own boundaries.
- The organization creates reusable tools, datasets, context assets, standards, playbooks, APIs, protocols, services, or learning systems.
- Other organizations obtain demonstrated value from the capability.
- Success is measured partly by how others adopt, improve, or move faster.
- The organization has legitimacy and credibility to support others.
- AI capability is distributed across leadership, product/program, operations, adoption, and technical functions.

### Key Bottleneck

**Infrastructure, interoperability, legitimacy, and sustainability.**

The organization can create value beyond itself but must ensure that others can safely rely on it without mission drift or harmful lock-in.

### Adoption Loop Focus

The learning loop expands across organizational boundaries. Evidence from adopters improves the infrastructure, standards, support model, and field knowledge.

### Context Maturity

The organization provides reusable or interoperable context, datasets, knowledge assets, benchmarks, standards, or protocols with:

- consent and governance;
- clear ownership;
- machine-readable access where useful;
- quality and provenance;
- maintenance and versioning;
- immediate valuable use cases;
- meaningful choice and exit paths.

### Stage 5 Guardrails

Stage 5 is not just scale ambition. It requires:

- demonstrated demand from other organizations;
- mission-control guardrails;
- sustainable funding;
- standards and governance;
- maintenance and incident capacity;
- distribution and implementation partnerships;
- clear ownership and accountability;
- partner and corporate influence boundaries;
- support model for adopters;
- interoperability and portability strategy;
- evidence that the infrastructure improves adoption or outcomes;
- credibility with the communities and organizations affected.

### Agentic Interfaces At Stage 5

Stage 5 organizations may expose APIs, protocols, or services for software agents as well as people when:

- real ecosystem use cases exist;
- permissions and identity are manageable;
- activity is observable;
- service reliability and costs are sustainable;
- adopters retain meaningful choice;
- human accountability remains clear.

Agentic accessibility is an architectural option, not a requirement for Stage 5.

### What They Need

- Infrastructure and maintenance funding.
- Productization support.
- Interoperability and protocol design.
- Open-source, openly specified, managed, or shared-access strategy as appropriate.
- Standards and governance support.
- Distribution and implementation partnerships.
- Adoption support for participating organizations.
- Cross-organization evidence and learning system.
- Mission protection and influence boundaries.
- Sustainable technical and service ownership.

### What Backfires

- Assuming every mature organization should become Stage 5.
- Building infrastructure without demonstrated demand.
- Treating funding as the only missing ingredient.
- Building without a maintenance and support model.
- Centralizing data or power unnecessarily.
- Forcing standardization where interoperability would be sufficient.
- Closed approaches that create harmful dependency.
- Corporate partnership drift.
- Trying to replace last-mile organizations instead of enabling them.

### Observable Indicators

- **Leadership and ownership:** accountability is distributed across leadership, adoption, product or program, operations, and technical functions.
- **People, workflows, and change:** internal capability and adopter support are strong enough to help other organizations change their own workflows.
- **Data and organizational context:** reusable or interoperable assets, APIs, standards, benchmarks, or knowledge systems have clear stewardship.
- **Technology, implementation, and resilience:** sustained infrastructure and service capability, transparent architecture, partner boundaries, portability, and credible continuity.
- **Governance, risk, and accountability:** shared standards, safety, monitoring, incident response, mission protection, and sector trust.
- **Evidence and learning:** cross-organization adoption and outcome evidence improve the shared capability and support model.
- **Resources and sustainability:** sustained infrastructure funding or a credible revenue, maintenance, and adopter-support path.

### Pro-Bono Rule Of Thumb

At Stage 5, pro-bono alone is not a sufficient basis for shared infrastructure. Long-term ownership, maintenance funding, governance, and adopter support must exist.

## 6. Adoption Capacity Domains

The stages provide the public narrative. The seven capacity domains show what the organization can strengthen, what is blocking its next healthy step, and what support may help.

Each domain is intentionally supportable. An organization should be able to respond to a weak domain by seeking a service, program, expert, partner, or funding mechanism rather than receiving only a descriptive label.

| Capacity domain | Diagnostic question | Typical support pathways |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leadership and ownership | Is AI adoption connected to strategy, sponsored by leadership, and owned by someone with sufficient time, authority, and accountability? | Leadership coaching, AI strategy, portfolio prioritization, fractional AI or adoption lead, governance setup. |
| People, workflows, and change | Can staff understand, question, and adopt AI-supported work, and are users, tasks, roles, review, exceptions, and human judgment being redesigned together? | Literacy training, user research, service and workflow design, organizational design, role redesign, change management. |
| Data and organizational context | Are the necessary data, knowledge, values, policies, strategy, and domain context usable, governed, owned, and maintained? | Data-readiness audit, knowledge management, data governance, context preparation, information architecture. |
| Technology, implementation, and resilience | Can the organization select, integrate, test, secure, maintain, and replace the technology while managing vendor, model, partner, cost, and continuity risk? | Vendor selection, solution architecture, technical implementation, senior QA, security review, maintenance, interoperability, migration, and handoff planning. |
| Governance, risk, and accountability | Are consequences, affected people, human accountability, safeguards, escalation, appeals, and incident response appropriate to the workflow's scope and risk? | Responsible-AI advisory, privacy and legal review, safeguarding, risk assessment, governance design, incident planning. |
| Evidence and learning | Are there baselines, intended outcomes, adoption measures, evaluations, failure reviews, and a cadence for improvement? | Measurement design, monitoring and evaluation, AI evaluations, research, learning-system design. |
| Resources and sustainability | Are funding, protected time, procurement, operating costs, maintenance capacity, and a credible long-term model available? For an organization that earns revenue, this includes whether that revenue covers the costs it is meant to cover. | Grants, budgeting, procurement support, business-model design, implementation-capacity funding, operating-cost planning. |

### How To Read The Domains

The framework should use a simple green / yellow / red judgment for each domain:

- **Green:** sufficient for the organization's current stage, intended scope, and risk.
- **Yellow:** usable but likely to constrain the next Adoption Loop or make deployment fragile.
- **Red:** a binding constraint that should be addressed before the intended next step.

A domain is red when it is missing a condition that the organization's next step requires. The required conditions are:

- the Stage 3 completion signals, for an organization at Stage 2 or Stage 3;
- the Stage 4 operating gates, for each workflow the organization is putting into operation or already operates;
- the additional gates for beneficiary or external exposure, whenever AI affects the people the organization serves, at any stage;
- the absence of every fragility flag: a flag that is present counts as a missing condition.

Each condition belongs to one domain. A red domain does not lower the organization's stage. Each missing condition is a binding constraint. The assessment names at most three, and gaps in the additional gates for beneficiary or external exposure come first. The line between yellow and green remains the assessor's judgment.

This is not a universal scorecard. A low-risk internal workflow and a high-risk beneficiary-facing service require different levels of capacity. The assessment should identify the two or three binding constraints rather than average weaknesses into one composite score.

Three important concepts remain outside the domain model:

- **Adoption depth and repeatability** are evidence used to determine stage.
- **Deployment scope** remains an independent descriptor because it changes accountability and support needs.
- **Ecosystem ambition** remains part of the aspiration profile and Stage 5 eligibility test.

### Technology Resilience And Dependency

Within the technology, implementation, and resilience domain, the assessment should examine:

- concentration on one model, vendor, platform, person, or partner;
- ownership and portability of data, prompts, context, workflows, and evaluations;
- pricing and consumption-cost exposure;
- availability of model or vendor substitutes;
- reliance on donated credits or pro-bono labor;
- technical and contractual ability to migrate;
- continuity if a partner leaves;
- fallback and rollback options;
- whether open-source or self-hosted alternatives are operationally realistic.

The goal is not independence at any cost. It is managed dependency with meaningful choice and exit paths.

### Evidence Maturity

Evidence should mature with adoption:

| Stage | Typical evidence maturity |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Beliefs, concerns, and peer examples. |
| 2 | Anecdotes, observed usefulness, and documented failures. |
| 3 | Workflow baselines, intended outcomes, adoption measures, and regular learning reviews. |
| 4 | Operational performance, user behavior, outcome evidence, failure monitoring, and improvement decisions. |
| 5 | Cross-organization adoption, comparative evidence, infrastructure reliability, and shared learning. |

## 7. Fragility Flags

Fragility flags are cross-domain warnings, not separate maturity layers. They should be grouped by the capacity that can address them.

### Leadership And Ownership

- no AI adoption owner or executive sponsor;
- owner lacks authority or protected time;
- project manager or champion left without succession.

### People, Workflows, And Change

- weak staff, user, or beneficiary participation;
- AI is added to tasks without workflow or role redesign;
- no training, adoption, or change-support plan.

### Data And Organizational Context

- unorganized, inaccessible, or unowned data and context;
- no maintenance responsibility or provenance;
- critical context exists only in individual files or vendor platforms.

### Technology, Implementation, And Resilience

- no technical owner or reliable senior partner;
- pro-bono dependency without quality assurance or handoff;
- single-vendor, single-model, single-person, or single-partner dependency;
- unknown consumption-cost exposure;
- no migration, rollback, fallback, maintenance, or continuity path.

### Governance, Risk, And Accountability

- sensitive or beneficiary-facing exposure without proportional governance;
- no explicit human accountability, escalation, appeal, or incident owner;
- security, privacy, safeguarding, or consequence risks have not been assessed.

### Evidence And Learning

- no baseline, intended outcome, or adoption measure;
- output volume is treated as impact;
- no failure review or learning cadence after deployment.

### Resources And Sustainability

- no protected implementation time;
- no tool, credit, operating, or maintenance budget;
- deployment depends on temporary funding with no continuation plan.

Example:

> Stage 3 Hybrid organization. Mission-critical internal pilot. Moderate risk. Leadership and context are green. People and workflows, technology resilience, and evidence are red. The pilot is fragile due to no technical owner, no workflow baseline, and dependence on a single pro-bono partner.

## 8. Support Pathways

The assessment should translate every red or yellow capacity domain into a concrete support recommendation. The provider may be TTTR, another nonprofit intermediary, a consultant, a funder, a technology company, a research partner, a pro-bono team, or a hired staff member.

A useful recommendation specifies:

- the capacity gap;
- the support type;
- the appropriate provider profile;
- the expected capacity outcome;
- the evidence that the gap has closed.

Examples:

- **Leadership and ownership:** fund a fractional adoption lead who establishes governance, prioritization, and a 90-day operating rhythm.
- **People, workflows, and change:** engage a service-design and change partner to redesign one priority workflow with staff and users.
- **Data and organizational context:** run a data and knowledge-readiness sprint that assigns ownership and produces a maintained context asset.
- **Technology, implementation, and resilience:** add senior architecture and QA support, with documentation, cost visibility, portability, and a handoff plan.
- **Governance, risk, and accountability:** commission a proportional risk, privacy, safeguarding, and human-accountability review before external deployment.
- **Evidence and learning:** establish a baseline, intended outcome, evaluation method, and monthly learning review for the selected workflow.
- **Resources and sustainability:** secure protected staff time, operating budget, procurement support, and a post-pilot maintenance model.

Product credits do not create implementation capacity. Pro-bono construction does not create ownership. Training alone does not redesign a workflow. Funding should address the full adoption system around the technology.

## 9. Optional Project Readiness Note

The framework does not add project maturity as a second core stage system. Many social impact organizations will deploy AI across several workflows rather than pursue one flagship project.

When an organization is pursuing a major AI project, the assessment should still ask:

> Is this project ahead of the organization's adoption capacity?

Lightweight guidance:

- Stage 1 organizations should not be pushed into a build project.
- Stage 2 organizations may use low-risk prototypes as learning devices.
- Stage 3 organizations can run pilots when ownership, time, context, workflow understanding, basic governance, and evidence plans exist.
- Stage 4 organizations can operate mission-critical AI only with the full operating gates appropriate to risk and scope.
- Stage 5 organizations can provide infrastructure others depend on only with demand, legitimacy, governance, evidence, maintenance, funding, support, and mission protection.

A project can be technically advanced while the organization remains adoption-fragile.

## 10. Recommended Stage Transitions

### Stage 1 → Stage 2: Resisting To Exploring

Organizations begin experimenting when:

- trusted peers demonstrate responsible use;
- staff see AI improve a real pain point;
- leadership gives permission to try safely;
- AI is framed as mission support rather than human replacement;
- initial privacy and values concerns receive practical answers.

### Stage 2 → Stage 3: Exploring To Systematizing

Organizations begin systematizing when:

- fragmented use becomes visible;
- champions can demonstrate wins and failures;
- leadership recognizes the need for shared guidance and ownership;
- several workflows show enough value to justify coordinated adoption;
- a program or funder creates protected time and a forcing function.

Stage 2 organizations usually need readiness and adoption support, not a heavy build program.

### Stage 3 → Stage 4: Systematizing To Innovating

Organizations move into Stage 4 when:

- they identify a mission-critical workflow worth redesigning;
- they can run the full Adoption Loop;
- accountable adoption and technical ownership exist;
- workflow roles and human judgment are redesigned;
- context is maintained and governed;
- risk and dependency are understood;
- evidence shows operational value;
- monitoring, maintenance, and funding are credible.

Beneficiary-facing deployment is not required. If it exists, the additional exposure gates apply.

### Stage 4 → Stage 5: Innovating To Enabling

Organizations move toward ecosystem enablement when:

- their capability works reliably for their own users;
- other organizations demonstrate demand;
- reuse fits the mission;
- the organization has legitimacy to support others;
- it can distribute, maintain, govern, and support the capability;
- interoperability and exit paths are credible;
- cross-organization evidence and learning are possible;
- sustainable funding exists.

Stage 5 should be chosen, not assumed.

## 11. Assessment Output

A practical v7 assessment should return:

1. Current adoption stage.
2. Healthy aspiration profile.
3. Deployment scope.
4. Risk exposure.
5. Seven-domain capacity profile using green, yellow, and red judgments appropriate to the intended scope and risk.
6. Two or three binding constraints and relevant fragility flags.
7. Recommended support pathway for each binding constraint, including the expected capacity outcome and evidence of progress.
8. Recommended next Adoption Loop intervention.
9. Optional project-readiness note.

Example:

> Stage 3 Hybrid organization. Healthy aspiration: Stage 4 for two mission-critical internal workflows. Current scope: shared internal with one mission-critical pilot. Moderate risk. Leadership and context are green. People and workflows, technology resilience, and evidence are red. Binding constraints: no technical owner, no workflow baseline, and dependence on one pro-bono partner. Recommended support: a service-design partner, senior technical QA with a portability and handoff plan, and a 90-day measurement-and-learning cycle before operational deployment. Next Adoption Loop: redesign and validate one priority workflow, then reassess deployment readiness.

## 12. Core v7 Message

The compressed v7 thesis:

> AI adoption in social impact organizations is an operating-model transition, not a tool rollout.

The practical v7 line:

> Stage 3 builds the AI adoption operating system. Stage 4 turns selected mission-critical workflows into reliable, governed, evidence-producing capabilities. Stage 5 turns proven capabilities into mission-protected infrastructure others can depend on.

The strategic implication:

> The winners will not be the organizations with the most AI tools or demos. They will be those that make adoption repeatable, governed, economically sustainable, and cumulative.

The road consists of:

- leadership and accountable ownership;
- people, workflows, and change;
- data and organizational context;
- resilient technology and implementation;
- proportional governance and human accountability;
- evidence and learning loops;
- resources and sustainability that survive the pilot.
