# AI Adoption Framework v7: what changes

A draft by the framework's authors, Jacek Siadkowski and Radosław Taraszka (Tech To The Rescue). First draft 28 September 2026; revised 30 September and 5 October 2026 by Radosław Taraszka, on 9 October in answer to Jacek Siadkowski's review of 8 October, and corrected on 10 and 15 October. Nothing here is in force: the released version is v6 (6.0.1).

The proposed text is [`v7-draft.md`](v7-draft.md). It starts as a byte-for-byte copy of `v6.md` (6.0.1), so every change shows against v6. This note says what changes and why. Review comments and the decisions taken on them are in [`v7-feedback.md`](v7-feedback.md).

## Summary

v7 keeps the five stages, the seven capacity domains, the colours and the Adoption Loop. It changes three things:

1. Every stage opens with one placement table: what places an organization there, what it requires, and the question that checks each row. Everything else is a readiness checklist. v6 said this in part for Stages 3 and 4, with a different kind of list for each stage.
2. Some rules change, so an organization's result can change. That makes v7 a major release, with a crosswalk from the v6 identifiers.
3. The text is reorganized for reading. The conditions keep their words.

## Changes that can move an organization's result

### Stage placement (§2, §5)

| Stage | Reached when | Required | What v6 said |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 Resisting | The organization does not use AI in its work, and nobody owns its adoption. | Nothing. | A description of attitudes: skepticism, concerns, AI seen as a threat. |
| 2 Exploring | People use AI in their own work, however unevenly, or someone owns its adoption. | Nothing more. | A description, with no rule. |
| 3 Systematizing | The organization runs AI pilots on selected workflows, or has run the Adoption Loop on them more than once. | • A named AI owner, champion, or adoption group with time and authority.<br>• People share what works and what fails.<br>• AI use is expected in selected areas. | "Most of" thirteen completion signals, the owner being one of them. |
| 4 Innovating | AI is integral to a workflow that matters to mission delivery or organizational performance: if it failed or went wrong for a week, the people served would be harmed, or a core operation would stop. | • The workflow runs in normal operations.<br>• It is redesigned, with explicit roles.<br>• It has accountable, technical, and data owners.<br>• Evidence improves it.<br>• Governance, maintenance, dependency, and failure handling are active practices. | Seven defining conditions and fifteen operating gates the organization "should have", without saying which are required. |
| 5 Enabling | At least two independent organizations use, in their own work, a tool, dataset, standard, playbook, or service the organization created. | • Other organizations obtain demonstrated value from it.<br>• Sustainable funding.<br>• Maintenance and incident capacity. | Twelve guardrails, all required. |

An organization is at the highest stage it reaches whose requirements it meets, together with the requirements of every stage below. Everything else in a stage profile is a readiness checklist: an unmet item is a gap to close and never lowers the stage.

The last column of each placement table in §5 is the question that checks the row. An assessment tool may word it its own way.

Why each stage changes:

- **Stage 1** is placed by what the organization does, because an assessment cannot establish attitudes reliably. Skepticism and concerns stay in the profile as typical traits.
- **Stage 2** also takes an organization where someone owns AI adoption but nobody uses AI yet. It is no longer resisting.
- **Stage 3** requires the owner, champion, or adoption group named in v6's own definition. Its completion signals listed an owner as one of thirteen and asked for "most" of them, so an organization could reach Stage 3 with nobody leading adoption. It also requires that people share what works and what fails, which v6 lists among the conditions for moving to Stage 3 ("champions can demonstrate wins and failures", v6 §10). And it requires S3.def.1, that AI use becomes expected in selected areas rather than exceptional; one team or function is enough. Without it, one pilot with an owner would reach Stage 3, which v6 rules out: stage progression is "not the completion of one pilot" (v6 §4).
- **Stage 4** is decided by its definition and two of its gates, set out in one table. In short: AI is deployed and governed in a crucial internal or important beneficiary-facing workflow. A workflow is crucial or important when it passes a test: if it failed or went wrong for a week, the people served would be harmed, or a core operation would stop. "Governed" does not include the gates for the people served; a missing gate makes a domain red and never lowers the stage.
- **Stage 5** requires a capability others use, not only one the organization produces: at least two independent organizations use it in their own work and obtain demonstrated value from it. v6 already calls "building infrastructure without demonstrated demand" a mistake. Two organizations, not one, because one may be a single client; a second shows the capability is reusable. Sustainable funding, and maintenance and incident capacity, must cover that capability. The other nine guardrails become a readiness checklist. This relaxes v6, which required all twelve. Most of the nine, such as boundaries on corporate influence or credibility with affected communities, need an expert's judgment rather than a fact an assessment can check.
- **The gates for beneficiary or external exposure** never lower the stage. When AI affects the people the organization serves, a missing gate makes its domain red.

### Red means a required condition is missing (§6)

v6 defines green, yellow and red but does not say how to decide between them. v7 adds a rule for red: a domain is red when it is missing a condition the organization's next step requires. Each such condition belongs to one domain; the proposed assignment is in [`v7-domains.md`](v7-domains.md). The assessment names at most three binding constraints, gaps in the gates for the people the organization serves first. The line between yellow and green stays the assessor's judgment.

A small organization at Stage 2 that drafts its newsletter with AI can be red in Technology for lacking a view of its dependence on vendors and partners, an item of the Stage 3 checklist. It is not red for lacking a plan to leave its AI provider, a Stage 4 gate, because it runs no AI workflow in operation.

### Social impact organizations, not only nonprofits (title, §1, §6, §9, §11)

The framework is already used to assess social enterprises and impact ventures. v7 says so: the title becomes "AI Adoption Framework for Social Impact Organizations", and §1 defines social impact organizations as nonprofits, social enterprises, and impact ventures. §8 names kinds of support provider, such as a nonprofit intermediary, and no single provider.

The _Resources and sustainability_ question gains one sentence: for an organization that earns revenue, whether that revenue covers the costs it is meant to cover. An invented social cooperative runs a café that prices meals below cost on purpose, because a grant pays for employing people with disabilities. Its revenue covers what it is meant to cover, so the planned subsidy does not count against it.

## Changes to the text only

- **Stage profiles (§5)** have three parts: Placement, What it looks like, and What helps. "What Defines This Stage" is split between what places the organization, what it requires, and typical traits. The completion signals, operating gates and guardrails are each called the readiness checklist. Labels in the text replace most sub-headings.
- **Observable indicators** move from each stage to one table per domain in §6, so each domain reads across the five stages in one place.
- **Context maturity and Evidence Maturity fold into §6.** Each repeated the indicators of one domain; what they added moves into §6, below the Data and organizational context table and into the Evidence and learning rows. The example in §7 is removed, because §10 gives it in full.
- **§9** no longer asks for "the full operating gates" at Stage 4, or for all of legitimacy, governance and mission protection at Stage 5. These are readiness checklist items.
- **§10 of v6, Recommended Stage Transitions, is removed.** Its lists repeat the stage profiles, and they were one more place defining what Stage 4 needs. Its identifiers are retired; the table below says where each item lives in v7.
- **Two checklist items are removed** because they repeat a requirement in other words: S3.signal.1 repeats S3.def.3, and S4.gate.4 repeats S4.def.2 and S4.def.3.
- **One Stage 1 trait is removed:** S1.def.3, "Little direct use inside the organization", contradicts what places an organization at Stage 1, that it does not use AI. An organization with a little use is at Stage 2.
- **Each stage's "Adoption Loop focus" is removed.** It repeated the list at the end of §4 of which steps each stage runs.
- **§6 uses the §5 names:** the readiness checklists and the gates for the people the organization serves. The rule for red still covers S4.gate.3 and S4.gate.5, which were operating gates and are now Stage 4 requirements.
- **§6 and §10** say at most three binding constraints, as the rule for red does, not "two or three".
- A style pass over the text v7 adds; v6 prose and the conditions keep their words.
- **§11 of v6, TTTR Program Implications, is removed.** It mapped the stages to Tech To The Rescue's own programs; §8 already describes support by type, from any provider. Tech To The Rescue keeps the mapping in its own materials, and v6 stays published whole. §12 and §13 of v6 become §10 and §11.
- "Version 6" and "v6" become "Version 7" and "v7", and the header marks the text as a draft.

## Open questions

1. Do these changes keep what v6 intends?
2. Is the wording of what places an organization at Stage 3 right? No decision is behind it yet.
3. For S3.def.1, "selected areas": is one team or function enough, or should it be several?
4. Stage 5: is two independent organizations the right number?
5. A missing placement requirement lowers the stage. Should it also make its domain red (§6)? The rule for red covers the readiness checklists and the gates for the people the organization serves, and of the requirements only S4.gate.3 and S4.gate.5.
6. [`v7-domains.md`](v7-domains.md) marks seventeen conditions whose domain is disputed. Is the proposed domain right for each?
7. How should an assessor tell yellow from green? v7 leaves this to the assessor's judgment; we propose settling it in v8.

## An example

Take an invented food bank that uses AI to decide who gets an emergency food parcel. It has rules for AI and monitors it. A named person is accountable for mistakes, another keeps its eligibility rules and family records up to date, and staff check every refusal and share what they learn across teams. But a family the AI refuses cannot find out why, and has no one to ask to look again. The food bank also has no plan for losing its AI provider.

Under v6, nobody can say what colour its _Governance, risk, and accountability_ domain should be, or whether it is at Stage 3 or Stage 4.

Under v7:

- The food bank is at Stage 4. Allocating food parcels passes the test of a workflow the mission depends on, and the food bank meets what Stage 4 requires.
- Its governance domain is red. AI shapes a decision about each family, so the family needs a way to appeal. This is the food bank's first binding constraint. It does not lower the stage: the appeal route is one of the gates for the people served, not part of "governed".
- Its technology domain is red. The missing plan for losing its provider is a gap to close in a workflow it already operates. It does not lower the stage.

If nobody at the food bank had the time or authority to lead its AI adoption, it would be at Stage 2.
